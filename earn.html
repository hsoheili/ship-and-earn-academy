The Earning Path — Your First Paid Web Work

Building the site is half of it. The other half is finding someone who needs one, agreeing on a price, doing the work, and getting the money into your account. That half is a skill too. Nobody teaches it to you in a tutorial, so we teach it here.

Nothing on this page is a promise. Everything on this page is followed by the work it takes.

Four realistic routes

These are the four ways our students have actually been paid. Pick one to start. Two at once is usually one too many, and the fourth route only works well after you have done some of the first three.

  • 1. Small local client sites

    A barber, a dog groomer, a tutoring collective, a food truck. They need five pages, a phone number people can tap, and hours that are actually correct. You need clean HTML and CSS, a domain, a host, and the nerve to walk in and ask.

    Work it takes: about 20 in-person or email conversations to land your first two paid jobs. Most students hear no eight or nine times first.

  • 2. Freelance marketplace gigs

    Small, sharply defined jobs: fix a broken layout, make this page work on a phone, add a contact form, speed up a slow page. You need to read a brief carefully, estimate honestly, and write English a stranger trusts.

    Work it takes: 30 to 60 proposals before your first win, and a profile that shows three real projects. Early gigs pay badly. They buy you reviews.

  • 3. Subcontracting for agencies

    A small studio has more work than hands. They hand you the design and you build the pages. You need to follow someone else's conventions, hit dates, and communicate before you are late rather than after.

    Work it takes: a portfolio that proves you can build to a spec, plus one warm introduction. This route rarely comes from cold email. It comes from meetups and from people you have already helped.

  • 4. Selling templates or small tools

    A landing page template, a print-friendly invoice page, a small accessible component set. You need real polish, documentation a stranger can follow, and patience — this pays nothing for weeks and then trickles.

    Work it takes: 40 to 80 hours to make one thing genuinely good, and a year of small updates. Best treated as a second income stream, not a first.

Pricing your work

The first quote is the scariest email you will send. Here is the method we teach, and it is boring on purpose.

How to quote a first project

Write down every task, including the dull ones: gathering text, resizing photos, setting up the domain, a round of changes, the handoff call. Add up your honest hours. Multiply by 1.4, because you will be wrong. Then price the whole project as one number, not an hourly rate — clients understand "$550 for the five-page site" far better than "$25 an hour, roughly 22 hours."

Common beginner mistakes

  • Quoting before scoping

    You say a number on the spot, then discover they want an online shop. Always ask for the page list in writing first. "Let me send you a proper number tomorrow" is a complete sentence.

  • Charging by sympathy

    Discounting because the client is nice, or small, or a friend of your aunt. Price the work, then decide separately whether you want to give a discount and say so out loud.

  • Unlimited revisions

    Two rounds of changes is normal and generous. Round three onward is billed. Write that in the proposal before anyone is annoyed.

  • Forgetting the invisible hours

    Content chasing eats more time than coding on small sites. Budget three hours for "waiting on their photos and writing the text they promised."

The pitch kit

Three documents. We write them with you in a live session, then you edit them yourself until they sound like you and not like us.

The portfolio page

One page, three projects, no carousel. For each project: who it was for, what problem it solved, what you built, and how long it took. A line like "made the booking phone number tappable, calls went from 4 a week to 11" beats any screenshot gallery. If you have no clients yet, build for a real organisation for free and say so honestly — a school club, a neighbour's market stall, a community garden.

The one-page proposal

Our template has seven parts: what you understood from our conversation, the page list, what is not included, the price, the payment schedule, the timeline, and what you need from the client and when. It fits on one page because clients read one page. Longer documents get "I'll look at this later."

The cold outreach message

Six sentences maximum. Name something specific and true about their current site or their lack of one. Say what you would change first and why it matters to their customers. Give one price or a range. Ask for a fifteen-minute call. Sign with your name, your phone number, and a link to your portfolio page. No attachments, no "I hope this email finds you well."

I sent the six-sentence version to eleven shops on one street. Two replied, one hired me for $450, and the other one hired me four months later for $1,200. The long, polite email I sent before that got zero replies from thirty people.

Nadia R., second cohort, now building sites part-time in Oakland

Getting paid and staying legal

This is the part beginners skip and then regret. It takes one afternoon to set up and it protects you for years.

Deposits

Take 40% before you start, always, including from friends. A client who will not pay a deposit will not pay the final invoice either. This one rule has saved our students more money than any pricing advice.

Invoices

An invoice needs a number, a date, your name and address, their name and address, a plain description of the work, the amount, the due date, and how to pay. We give you a simple HTML invoice you can print to PDF, so you are never blocked waiting for software.

Simple contracts

Two pages, plain language: what you will deliver, what it costs, when payments are due, how many revision rounds are included, who owns the files at the end, and how either side can walk away. Email agreement on a written scope is far better than nothing, and a signed page is better still.

Taxes and local rules

Freelance income is taxable income in most places, even when you are a student and even when it is small. Keep every invoice and every receipt in one folder from day one. Rules differ enormously by country, state, and visa status, and student status can affect what you are allowed to earn.

We are not accountants or lawyers and we will not pretend to be. What we do is show you what to keep, what questions to ask, and where to look — your country's revenue agency site, your university's student enterprise office, or a local small-business advice clinic. Many of them are free.

Client work skills

Code you can look up. These you have to practise on real people, so we role-play them in cohort sessions before you meet a client.

  • Scoping

    Turning "I need a website" into a numbered list of pages and features. The trick is asking what a visitor should be able to do, not what the site should look like. Write the list, send it, ask them to confirm it in writing.

  • Saying no

    No to the shop that wants payments and stock levels on your second project. No to the friend who wants it by Friday for free. We give you three polite scripts, including a referral line so you leave the door open.

  • Handling revisions

    Ask for all changes in one list, not one email at a time. Quote a small fee for the third round, calmly, before you do the work. Vague feedback gets one question back: "which part, and what should it do instead?"

  • Scope creep

    "Could you also just…" is the most expensive phrase in freelancing. The answer is yes, happily, as a small add-on with its own price. Not a fight — a line item.

  • Handing off cleanly

    You will not support this site forever, so build for the person who comes after you. Domain and host logins in the client's own name, a one-page written guide to editing text and hours, and a plain list of what runs where.

  • Ending well

    Final invoice, handoff notes, and one email asking for a short testimonial and an introduction to one other business owner. Nearly half of our students' second clients came from that single email.

What separates the students who keep going

We asked. It was not talent, and it was not free time. Four things showed up again and again:

  • They pitch on a schedule

    Five outreach messages a week, on the same day, whether they feel like it or not. Mood is a terrible manager.

  • They finish ugly

    A shipped site that could be nicer beats a beautiful one still on their laptop. Perfectionism looks like diligence and behaves like avoidance.

  • They ask for the referral

    Every single time, at the end of every job. It is one sentence and it compounds faster than any skill.

  • They show up to the meetup

    Monthly, in Oakland or on the call. Subcontracting work and warm introductions come from rooms, not inboxes.
